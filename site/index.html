Store Sales · Time Series Forecasting

Predicting the next 16 days of grocery sales, explained step by step

A grocery chain wants to know how many units of each product every store will sell over the next two weeks, so it can stock the shelves without wasting food or running out. This page shows how we built that forecast, why each choice was made, and how five different algorithms compared. No machine-learning background needed: hard words are explained in the glossary.

Three things to take away

  1. The best forecast came from combining two similar models. Averaging a tuned LightGBM with a LightGBM trained separately for each forecast day scored 0.382.
  2. Cleaning the data helped more than fancier algorithms. Removing the zeros recorded before a store or product started selling was the single biggest gain (see step 6).
  3. The neural network (LSTM) did not win. Tree-based models were clearly better here, and adding the LSTM to the average made the result worse.

Step by step

How the forecast was built

Eight steps, in the order they happened. Each one says what we did and what we learned.

  1. 1

    Understand the problem

    What we did
    Read the competition rules. We must predict daily unit sales for every store × product family for the 16 days after the training data ends. Forecasts are scored with RMSLE.
    What we learned
    There are 54 stores × 33 product families = 1,782 separate sales histories ("series"), with 3,000,888 daily records from 2013-01-01 to 2017-08-15. RMSLE rewards getting proportions right, so slow-selling products count as much as big sellers.
  2. 2

    Explore the data

    What we did
    Plotted sales by weekday, month and year, looked at promotions, holidays, the oil price and the file of daily transactions.
    What we learned
    Sales follow a weekly rhythm (Saturday and Sunday are highest) and grow over the years. Promotions raise sales. 31% of the records are zero, and about half of those zeros come from before a product or store began selling. Christmas Day is missing every year, and the transactions file stops on the last training day, so it cannot be used.
    See the data →
  3. 3

    Prepare the target

    What we did
    Trained on log(1 + sales) instead of raw sales, then converted predictions back.
    What we learned
    Raw sales are extremely lopsided (a few huge values; skewness 7.4). After the log it is mild (0.41). The score is computed on this log scale too, so training on it directly optimises what is measured.
  4. 4

    Set up a fair test

    What we did
    Hid the last 16 days of the training data (2017-07-31 to 2017-08-15) and used them as an exam. Every model learns only from earlier days, and every "memory" feature (sales a few weeks ago) looks back at least 16 days, so nothing is peeked at.
    What we learned
    All numbers on this page are measured on the same 16 days, so models can be compared directly. The catch: it is only one window, so small differences should not be over-read.
  5. 5

    Build a baseline

    What we did
    Trained a first LightGBM model with calendar, promotion, holiday, oil price, store information and "sales N days ago" features.
    What we learned
    It scored 0.388. This is the yardstick: every later idea must beat it.
    Baseline: 0.388
  6. 6

    Fix the data and the features

    What we did
    Tried three ideas: (a) drop the zeros recorded before a series started selling, (b) add richer holiday features, (c) train a separate model for each forecast day.
    What we learned
    (a) helped the most: 0.390 → 0.386. (b) did not help, so we dropped it. (c) was better on nearby days but worse on far days, so it became an ingredient for step 8 rather than a replacement.
    See what helped →
  7. 7

    Compare algorithms and tune them

    What we did
    Trained LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, Random Forest and an LSTM neural network, then searched for better settings ("tuning") for the four tree-based ones.
    What we learned
    Gradient-boosted trees beat the rest. Tuning moved each algorithm by only about 0.002–0.003 in the search, so the defaults were already close to the best.
    Meet the models →
  8. 8

    Combine and double-check

    What we did
    Averaged the tuned LightGBM with the per-forecast-day LightGBM (equal weights, in log space), then reviewed the caveats.
    What we learned
    The average scored 0.382, better than either model alone. Adding XGBoost, CatBoost, Random Forest or the LSTM did not help, because their forecasts are almost the same as LightGBM's.
    Final: 0.382

    Why this method

    The reasoning behind each choice

    What we deliberately did not do
    • We did not tune anything on the 16 days we must forecast; they have no answers.
    • We did not claim a leaderboard rank. This page only knows our own validation window.
    • We kept ideas that failed in the write-up (richer holidays, store-level features, a Ridge hybrid) so the results are not cherry-picked.
    FAQ & glossary

    Frequently asked questions

    What exactly is being predicted?

    For each of 54 stores and each of 33 product families (for example "BREAD/BAKERY" or "BEVERAGES"), the number of units sold on each of the 16 days from 2017-08-16 to 2017-08-31. That is 28,512 numbers.

    What does a score of 0.382 mean?

    RMSLE is the typical size of the miss measured on log(1 + sales). A rough reading: e0.382 ≈ 1.47, so a typical single store-product-day forecast is off by a factor of around 1.5 in relative terms. Single days are noisy, so this says nothing about how accurate monthly totals would be. Lower is better and 0 would be perfect.

    Is 0.382 good?

    We can only say it is better than our own starting point (0.388) on this validation window. We cannot say how it ranks on the Kaggle leaderboard, which uses different days and hidden answers. The improvement over the baseline is small and comes from one window.

    Why does the error grow with the forecast day?

    The further ahead you forecast, the older the latest information. For day 16 the newest sales the model may use are 16 days old. LightGBM's average score is about 0.380 on days 1–4 and about 0.399 on days 13–16.

    Why not use the most advanced model, such as deep learning?

    We did try one: an LSTM neural network. It scored 0.423, the worst of the five, and averaging it with LightGBM made the result worse. It was not tuned, so a better LSTM is possible, but for tables with strong "sales N days ago" columns, tree-based models were the better fit here.

    Doesn't dropping zeros ignore real zero-sales days?

    No. Only zeros before a store or product first sold anything are dropped (they mean "not open yet"). Zeros after that are kept, because they are real demand of zero.

    Why average two models instead of using the best one?

    The single model and the per-day models make different mistakes: the per-day models are better on day 1 and the single model is better on day 16. Averaging cancels part of each one's errors, which is why 0.382 beats both 0.385 and 0.387.

    Why did richer holiday features not help?

    The basic holiday and calendar features already capture most of the effect, so the extra detail (bridge days, work days, distance to a holiday) mostly added noise. The score moved from 0.38571 to 0.38595, which is a tiny loss.

    Why do the lines in the "error by forecast day" chart zigzag?

    Each point is measured on a single day (1,782 store-product records), not an average over many days, so individual days that are unusually hard or easy show up as bumps. It is not a smoothed trend.

    Can I trust these numbers?

    Treat them as a fair comparison, not a guarantee. All scores come from one 16-day window; the best blend was picked after looking at 11 combinations; CatBoost on the GPU gives slightly different results between runs. See Technical details → Limitations.

    Which models run on a GPU?

    XGBoost, CatBoost and the LSTM. LightGBM and Random Forest run on the CPU: the standard pip build of LightGBM has no GPU support.

    Technical details

    For people who want to reproduce or extend it

    Data and validation split
    Training data3,000,888 rows, 2013-01-01 to 2017-08-15 (1,684 days; Christmas Day is absent every year)
    Rows used to fitFrom 2015-01-01 onward (earlier years are not used)
    Validation window2017-07-31 to 2017-08-15 (16 days, 28,512 rows); models are fitted on days before it
    Final fitThe chosen settings are refitted on all rows up to 2017-08-15 before forecasting
    Forecast window2017-08-16 to 2017-08-31 (16 days × 1,782 series = 28,512 rows)
    Target and metricTrained on log(1 + sales); scored with RMSLE (root mean squared error on that scale)
    Features
    • Identity: store, product family, city, state, store type, store cluster
    • Promotion: items on promotion, and its 7-day mean
    • Calendar: weekday, day of month, month, year, day of year, payday (15th and month end), days to Christmas, the April 2016 earthquake period
    • Outside world: oil price (missing days interpolated) and its 7-day mean; national, regional and local holidays
    • History (all shifted by at least 16 days): sales 16, 17, 21, 28, 35 and 364 days earlier; rolling means over 7, 14, 28 and 56 days; 14-day rolling standard deviation
    • Not used: the transactions file, because it does not cover the forecast days

    In the s1/s2/d3 experiments the history columns are measured on a complete day grid and, for d3, from the last known day (lags of 7·⌈h/7⌉ days and up for forecast day h).

    s2s1 + richer holiday features (additional days, bridge days, work days, events, days to and since a holiday)
    d3s2 + 16 separate models, one per forecast day, each using history from the last known day

    Output files
    • preds_<name>.npz: arrays val (the 16 validation days) and test (the 16 forecast days), on the log(1 + sales) scale, ordered by store, family, date. Use np.expm1 to get sales back.
    • ensemble_preds.npz: predictions from ensemble_model.py plus the validation targets y_val.
    • best_params.json: the best settings found for each algorithm.
    • The scripts no longer write submission files; convert test with expm1 and pair it with Dataset/test.csv ids if you need one.
    Limitations
    • All scores come from one 16-day validation window. There is no rolling-origin cross-validation yet, so small gaps (like 0.0001) are noise.
    • The best blend was chosen after looking at 11 combinations, so it may look slightly better than it truly is.
    • CatBoost on the GPU gives slightly different results between runs: 0.39941 in the search versus 0.39998 in the final fit. The tables use the final fit.
    • The LSTM was not tuned and uses the older feature set without the leading-zero trim, so it is not a like-for-like comparison with the tuned tree models.
    • The XGBoost, CatBoost and Random Forest "first run" rows use the older feature set; the "tuned" rows use the trimmed set.
    • Not tested on Linux or macOS, and there is no requirements or lock file; use the versions listed above.
